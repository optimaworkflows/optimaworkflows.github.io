Two realistic outcomes

Same storm corridor. Two different deployment calls.

Same broad hail event. Two different ways of deciding where to send commercial crews and cranes.

Example A — deployment based mainly on storm maps and operator judgment

Strong hail reports, weak commercial fundamentals

  • Commercial envelope density in the hardest-hit corridors was lower than expected
  • Larger out-of-state crews had already saturated the best industrial parks
  • Commercial permit turnaround was significantly slower than anticipated
  • A higher-than-expected share of properties carried weaker insurance positions
ResultCrews and cranes committed to a lower-quality zone. Overhead continued, margins compressed, and capital stayed tied up longer.
Example B — deployment based on zone intelligence and scoring

Same corridor, crews sent only where it scored well

  • Several zones scored marginal or high-risk on density, competition, and permit latency
  • Two adjacent zones scored stronger on both current conditions and historical outcomes
  • Crews and cranes deployed only into the higher-scoring areas
  • Crane utilization stayed high, competition remained manageable
ResultLower-ROI zones screened out before mobilization. Crews, cranes, and capital focused on stronger opportunities, improving utilization and cash-flow potential.

The difference wasn't better luck. It was better information before the first crane rolled.

The real cost of incomplete decisions

Most commercial CAT companies still call it with half the data.

What's usually used

  • Hail size and wind speed
  • Basic storm path
  • Experience from previous seasons

What regularly gets missed

  • Commercial envelope density able to support crane overhead
  • Local emergency permit processing speed
  • Building code rigidity and required upgrades
  • Insurance endorsement mix (RCV vs. ACV)
  • Specialty labor and crane operator availability
  • Real competitor saturation on the ground
  • What happened last time a similar event hit a comparable zone

When these factors are ignored, the pattern repeats: crews arrive, the easy work is already contested, permits drag, margins shrink, and cash stays trapped longer than planned.
