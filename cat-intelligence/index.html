Two Realistic Outcomes

Same broad storm corridor. Two different ways of deciding where to send crews.

Example A — Deployed on Maps + Gut Feel

A regional CAT company saw strong hail reports across a metro corridor. The maps looked excellent. Crews were mobilized, housing secured, materials staged.

  • – Residential density in the hardest-hit corridors was lower than expected
  • – Larger out-of-state crews had already saturated the best neighborhoods
  • – Commercial permit turnaround was significantly slower than anticipated
  • – A higher-than-expected share of properties carried weaker insurance positions

Result: heavy overhead consumed, margins compressed, working capital tied up far longer than planned.

Capital impact on that single deployment cycle: low-to-mid six figures.

Example B — Used the Scoring System First

Another operator evaluated the same broad corridor. The system scored several zones as marginal or high-risk on density, competitor load, and permit latency, and referenced outcomes from comparable past events.

  • ✓ Two adjacent zones scored stronger on both current conditions and historical performance
  • ✓ Crews deployed only into the higher-scoring areas
  • ✓ Crew utilization stayed high, competition remained manageable
  • ✓ Cash conversion was materially faster

Result: avoided the low-ROI zones entirely, protected overhead that would otherwise have been at risk.

The difference wasn't better luck — it was better information before the first truck rolled.

The Real Cost of Incomplete Decisions

Most CAT companies still make go/no-go calls with only part of the data.

  • What's usually used: hail size and wind speed, basic storm path, experience from previous seasons.
  • What regularly gets missed: actual residential and commercial density that can support the overhead, local emergency permit processing speed, building code rigidity and required upgrades, insurance endorsement mix (RCV vs. ACV), specialty labor availability, real competitor saturation on the ground, and what happened the last time a similar event hit a comparable zone.

When these factors are ignored, the outcome is familiar: crews arrive, the easy work is already contested, permits drag, margins shrink, and cash stays trapped longer than planned.

What is a Standard Zone? One contiguous storm-impact area that forms a single logical deployment decision — typically one metro area or a cluster of up to 8–12 contiguous ZIP codes (roughly 15–25 miles across the core impact area). Example: Core DFW impact area (North Dallas, Richardson, Plano, parts of Garland/Addison) = one Standard Zone ($3,500 Mixed / $5,500 Commercial).
